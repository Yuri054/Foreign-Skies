# Foreign Skies: a player's guide

Foreign Skies is a sandbox of Europe from 1900 to the great war and after. Nothing ends it and nothing scores you: you
run a nation and the AI runs the rest. This guide says what the screens are for and how the pieces fit. The keys are in
the README and in the pause menu (Esc).

## Starting

On the main menu choose **New game**, a scenario (January 1900, at peace; or July 1914, with the crisis under way) and a
**difficulty**: *Historical* (the nations play as they were trained), *Challenging* (bolder, take worse odds, stand by their
allies, quarrel sooner) or *Sandbox* (slower to quarrel, quicker to make peace). The difficulty never gives anyone more than
the rules give; it retunes how the AI behaves. **Tutorial** is a guided first game in 1900. Music and sound live under
**Settings**.

Then pick a nation. Pause with Space; the game runs in real time at five speeds.

## The map

Left click selects a province, an army or a fleet; a box drag selects several; right click gives an order to what is selected,
or opens the country panel on another nation. The map has modes (keys 1 to 6): political, diplomacy (who likes whom, from any
nation's point of view), economy, population, military (control, front line, strength, **supply**) and terrain. **V** turns on
the 3D map, with real relief and a moving sea (the settings, the gear at the top right, can still the sea on a slower
graphics card). Hover a province for its card; with armies selected the mouse also shows what an order there would do (see
*Armies*).

## The Nation screen (N)

One window, a tab for each part of running the nation:

- **Overview**: the **cabinet briefing** (the three things most worth your attention, each with the reason and a button to
  the tab that deals with it), your figures, Europe's tension (what it is made of, the worst rivalries, a chart of two years)
  and your wars with what each is being fought for.
- **Economy (E)**: money, taxes, spending, industry, food, raw materials. Money pays the army, interest and what the state buys.
  Every nation begins with a small surplus, and the economy is for spending it: on buildings, which grow it, or on the
  army, which it keeps. The **Standing army** slider sets how many men you keep under arms in peace (up to a fifth of your
  manpower): each division costs a sum a month, a soldier as dear as your people's wages, and the page says how many more
  divisions your surplus keeps, how many the treasury can raise, how many your recruits fill and how many months the
  treasury pays for a full mobilization. Raise the standing army, and the recruits come in over the next months; then
  raise the divisions on the Military tab.
  **Arms production** puts a share of your industry into munitions for the guns, before buildings: it costs a little output
  and the stockpile costs money to keep, but in a war guns without shells fight much weaker. The page says how many days of
  war the stockpile lasts; in a war you can also buy munitions abroad, if the sea is open.
  **Taxes** above the usual 10% bring money and cost growth and the people's support, more with every point; under it they
  bring less and the economy grows faster. **State investment** puts a sum a month into the country's development, and
  it compounds: each pound invested a year adds about a pound to the yearly output, which brings more revenue to invest. **Mobilization** calls up the reserve: the slider says what it costs a month before you move
  it. Raw materials and food are bought abroad by themselves: they matter when a blockade cuts the sea.
- **Build (B)**: pick a building, then click a green province. **A building costs money when it is begun, then industry
  (IC)**: the nation has a number of IC a day, projects are served in order and wait when the day's IC runs out. The queue
  shows why a project is "stalled" (no IC reaches it). A small country has fewer IC, but its buildings cost less: they are
  built to the size of the province. Each building says what it costs in its best place now, what it adds to the budget a
  year and how many years the money takes to come back: a factory in a great city pays back in about ten years, one in a
  small town much later. Trenches and fortifications cost only IC.
- **Military (M)**, **Navy (Y)**: armies, army groups, fronts, fleets, yards.
- **Diplomacy (F)**: opinions, treaties, wars, gifts, demands, peace.
- **Focus (O)**: one national effort at a time, with lasting effects.
- **Rankings (R)**: every nation side by side, with charts.
- **History (H)**: the history of the run, year by year, the wars, the changes of the map and how nations stand; it can be
  exported as a document or a web page.

## Armies

Select armies and right-click a province. The mouse tip shows the days the road takes, the date of arrival, how much of the
way is out of supply and what you can see of an enemy there (a range from your intelligence, never the exact men, with the
odds on men alone: terrain and trenches are not counted). An army out of supply loses men; the **Supply** map variant shows
where your armies are fed (green), where supply thins (amber) and where it ends (red). Armies dig in the longer they stand
still; defenders in trenches and on rough ground are hard to dislodge, and an attacking division that enters an undefended
province takes it at once; cavalry takes ground after a battle in half the time and cuts down a beaten army as it falls
back. After a war, **Send home** (the command panel) sends an army's men back to the reserve and its
upkeep stops; the guns go last. It works in peace only.

**Battle Plans.** Select an army (its card, or a field marshal's to take the whole group) and the **Battle Plans**
toolbar over the command panel gives it orders; while a tool is in hand the pointer is a crosshair, a hint follows it,
and Esc puts the tool down. The map then shows only the selected armies' plans.

- **Front line**: click a border to put the armies on the stretch of it there; **Shift+click** for the whole border.
  Each army spreads its divisions along the line as **detachments**, one stack to a province, more where the enemy is
  massed; with several armies on one front each holds a stretch of its own, side by side, never mixed. **Cohesion**
  (the Military tab) says how far a division may be sent to fill a gap: *Rigid* two provinces, *Balanced* six,
  *Flexible* anywhere. A stack you send somewhere by hand stays on its front and is left alone until it gets there.
- **Offensive line**: drag across enemy ground to mark the line to push to (hold **Shift** to add it as the next phase).
  **Spearhead**: click the one province to break through at. Needs the army on a front.
- **Fallback line**: drag a line through your own ground; armies on it dig in two levels deeper and half again as
  fast (dashed on the map). **Garrison**: tick what to guard (ports, forts, cities, the capital) and click your
  provinces; Enter or Finish.
- **Naval invasion**: click a port of yours, then the coast to land on; the army gathers in the port and sails once a
  fleet there has the transports for it. **Naval support**: click a coast and your nearest free warships sail off it
  on Shore support, adding up to 30% to your attacks there. **Paradrop** stays off: there are no aircraft yet.
- **Unassign** takes the armies off their fronts and drops their plans. **Edit**: drag over a target line or a drawn
  line to add provinces or take them off (a spearhead's target moves to the province clicked). **Delete**: click an
  order on the map to remove it; right click the button to clear every order of the selection (or all of yours).
- **Execute** (the play button) launches the plans, or halts them so the armies plan again. Standing on its front
  with a plan an army **plans**: up to +30% to its attacks after two weeks, ready to go after one (a green tick on the
  banner); marching or fighting spends it. **Careful** (›) attacks only at two to one and stops when morale is low,
  **Balanced** (››) at fair odds, **Aggressive** (›››) at even odds, at once, whatever the morale.
- The **+** card at the end of the cards forms an army group of the selected armies.

A push sends each stack at the enemy province next to it that leads toward the line, stacks going for the same
province together; a spearhead gathers the whole army next to the next province on the way and strikes it together. On
the map a push is a wide arrow in the army's colour to its target line, the enemy ground between hatched; a spearhead is
a narrow arrow ringed at its target; a landing an arrow from its port over the sea; later phases are dashed. A yellow
triangle on a banner means low supply, a red "!" an army at war on no front. Selecting a stack outlines every stack of
its army in the army's colour; stacks marching to their place on a front draw their road in that colour, and a stretch
by rail is dashed with a small train. The Military tab lists the fronts and has the same plan controls.
Armies can be grouped under a field marshal.
In a battle you are in, the battle screen has a **staff report**: how it goes, how long each side lasts, and what a quarter
more power, a weaker enemy or another level of trench would do.

## Diplomacy

Almost everything costs **diplomacy points**, earned monthly (more with prestige): treaties (alliance, guarantee,
non-aggression, passage), gifts, improving relations, making a demand.

**War needs a reason.** A war with no casus belli costs prestige, goodwill and war support, and needs a mobilized country; on a
nation that stands apart from the blocs (no great-power ally, not mobilized) it costs more, and the whole of Europe grows tenser.
Better to have a goal:

- Some goals come with the scenario: a **claim** (France on Alsace-Lorraine), which gives a casus belli on its provinces; a
  defence of a guarantee or an ally.
- **Make a demand** (Diplomacy tab) is how a war gets its reason: choose what you ask of the nation (named provinces, or a
  concession: reparations, a withdrawal of its border armies, no new warships, an end to its ties to someone), pay the
  diplomacy points, and it has until a deadline to yield, refuse, mobilize (if you cannot win, you back down) or appeal to
  the powers. If it refuses you hold a casus belli with that demand as the goal of the war. At peace the goal is half
  price and everything else dearer, so a war for Liège is easy to end with Liège and hard to end with Brussels.

**Crises** also arise by themselves between rivals when tension and grievances build: a demand with a deadline. If it concerns
you, you are asked at once. If it does not, you may **mediate**, once (a chance from your prestige and the goodwill you hold with
both; success eases tension and earns prestige) or **back** a side. A war with many nations fought at once is closed at a
**conference**: your allies at war with the same enemy sit with you, each asks what its own goal and score pay for, and the
enemy takes the whole package or refuses (it may offer less, for a month).

**Peace terms** are paid for in war score. Land you take must touch your own, through the other provinces you take or across
a sea you hold or have a coast on, a great power gives up at most a quarter of its land at one peace, and a capital goes
only with the whole country: the peace screen says what is cut off. A
beaten enemy (its people will not fight on, or you hold its capital and its war support is spent, or you hold most of it)
gives more than your score pays for, but only the ground you hold, your war's goal and money. Land lost at a peace is not
forgotten: the loser keeps a claim to it for 25 years and resents the winner, and is likely to demand it back.

## Events and tension

Europe's **tension** (top bar) rises with the arms race, mobilization, rivalry and recent crises; the bolder the AI nations
grow, the nearer it gets to 100. Historical events stop time and put choices to you: each option shows its **largest effect**
and, on hover, everything it does. After the great war the world goes on: provinces lost at a peace, unrest in the empires and
conferences on armaments come from how things stand, not from the calendar. When your focus tree is done, its last foci can be
taken again. On the Overview you may set your nation an **ambition** (keep out of the great war, unify the nation, become a
great power): not a score, a direction, and the History book records how it went. In the Overview you can take a public stand, one every half year: a call for calm weighs as much as your nation
does (a great power's, when Europe is tense, can pull it back from the brink); rattling the sabre cheers your people and
annoys your neighbours.

## Saving

F5 and Shift+F5 quick-save and quick-load; F6 opens the save window. The game saves itself on the first of each month into three
rotating slots. A save keeps the difficulty it was made at.

## Tips

- Read the cabinet briefing each month; it is the quickest way to see what is wrong.
- Your effort compounds: build, then put the surplus into state investment, and the economy grows faster every year.
  Any nation, even the smallest, can climb to the top given time; each 1 January the news says how far you have come.
- Guns or growth: every pound on the army is a pound not compounding. Money that lies idle in peace buys nothing but a
  war chest.
- Do not send armies beyond supply without a plan to take ground that will carry them.
- A claim is cheaper than a pretext, and a goal makes the peace you want cheap.
- Allies pull you into wars as they pull the enemy's allies in: look at who is bound to whom before you declare.
